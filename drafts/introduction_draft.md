## Introduction

1. Why is it important to compare multivariate time series?
2. Current ways of represent multivariate time series facilitating comparison?
3. Limitations?

**Why is it important to compare multivariate timeseries?**

- There’s information that is encoded not in a single sensor or node that we are interested in understanding, and to do that, you need a method of analysis that is sensitive to the information that lies within the dependency structures between nodes, channels, sensors.
- Typically, people tend to represent it as a strength of coupling, for which there are diverse ways of doing this, for example, covariance, directed coherence, transfer entropy.
- It is important to compare multivariate time series, and the interesting structure is how different parts of the system interact, and there have been hundreds of methods developed to quantify the interaction in all different complicated ways, time-lags, nonlinearities, spectral dependency.
- This problem is important, it has attracted hundreds of methods across diverse literature of everyone trying to do it like this^ — *how do we capture the dependency structure?*

**What is the problem(s)?** 

- If $P$ (process size, or $M$)common, we can compare nodal adjacency matrices directly. How?
    - Many ways to take unique values and compare directly, e.g. entry-wise correlations, distance measures.
- If not common, you need to abstract to a representation of the system that allows for comparison.
    - Average coupling (mean + distribution + summary statistics of adjacency matrices)
    - Graphical, nodal topology abstraction (distribution of edges, degree distribution, modularity, centrality, etc), abstract into a network topology that, despite not being the same size, can be represented in a way that is comparable in the same space.
    - Crude (not really used too much): extrapolate, imputate, mutate, compress (mean, distribution, summaries).

**What is the problem? Even if** $P$ **common *and* matched sensors — what is problem?**

- E.g. wind measurements from the same locations geographically, or exact same brain regions, etc.
- What is a more fundamental thing that even P common and matched (e.g. exact regions correspond) cannot measure?
- Using these diverse methods, we can be well informed on the strength — under many diverse lenses — of the strength of coupling; but, we cannot quantify the nature of dependency structure.
- Imagine $P$ common and matched between two adjacency matrices, and we pick one statistic from our family of well-known statistics, e.g. covariance, transfer entropy, granger causality, phase lag index, etc., and then use some method of comparison (typically Pearson correlation, then measure correlation distance between two P \times P (M \times M) adjacency matrices) — what are the issues with this? What does it mean and what doesn’t it measure?
    - e.g. in dynamic functional connectivity, we have a recording, window into slices, and correlate every pair; for stationary, high correlation; for non-stationary, low, can track a trajectory.

**What’s the problem (main)?** Short: we (i) pick a method (e.g. Pearson correlation), which implicitly bakes in a \emph{how} we interact; and (ii) measure the strength of the interactions \emph{under that interaction lens only, a priori}. We can never get around the fact that we think the \emph{best} way to represent our system is the set of strengths, with a baked-in prior on how these strengths interact, and so we are blind to changes in how the system organisation — the coupling itself — may change. 

- (i) Current methods (almost every paper) pick a method (typically Pearson) and then measure correlation distance (between series) — it’s not wrong; it’s useful, it’s been used in thousands of papers…but in measuring correlation between the coupling strength we are exposed to the limitation that we have to **pick** one of the many families —> solution is pyspi, a comprehensive aggregation of many different methods from many different families.
- **By choosing a bespoke method, we as a researcher implicitly bake in a “how?” — we initialise an inductive bias prior through which we view *how* components of a/the complex system interact.**
- In the popular case where we choose Pearson correlation, we are baking in the fact that we are measuring *strength* of an a priori — *how*. We think system elements interact with no lag, linearly. And we think that we can capture changes in the system from those set of strengths (the adjacency matrix of system elements).
- For example, consider two sensors: in certain time windows, they interact very strongly; then in the next, they are a bit weaker, and the following they are super strong — it’s about *how strongly* they interact. This is the core of our representation. We pick a *way* they interact, and then we represent the system by *how strongly* they interact.
- This is how we are choosing to represent our system. There is a choice to represent the system in terms of **the strength of coupling** between all pairs of nodes under a given metric of coupling.
- (ii) Measure strength.
- Here [bake in] we impose [something], and it’s not solved by just picking a different statistic or lens under which we view our system because they *all* impose some prior. You can **never get around the fact that you think the best representation of your system is the set of strengths.**
    - For example, if you have an old person and a young person, were you to represent your system in this way, you are communicating that there is a specific pair/set (or every pair) of regions where how strongly they couple is representative of age. And this is true for whatever outcome you care about, i.e., high-IQ—low-IQ, dementia-vs-control, schizophrenia-vs-control.
    - As soon as you choose a representation of the raw system data that you will carry forward, all you are sensitive to is the relative difference in strengths, *under that one measure*, e.g., that one [specific nodal connection] is weaker in old people, that one is stronger.
    - And because you have eliminated the raw data, all you have is one set [a nodal adjacency matrix] according to a single way to represent strength. And so the only differences you could find are differences in strength between coupling.
    - If you were instead to abstract to a network topology, the question becomes “*is there a difference in how topologically strong connections and weak connections are organised? do they tend to form clusters, etc., …”*.
    - You are still stuck on the fundamental representation of structure being the strength of interaction between pairs of nodes, either how topologically they’re organised, or how individual strengths are higher or lower. Under this representation, this is the sort of outcome you could possibly measure once you have chosen that representation.
    - In particular, you could **never** measure if the \emph{character} of interaction has changed. Because you have gone from raw data, with dense, rich structure, to representing the system as only a set of strengths, you are blind to changes in the nature of the dependency itself. And once you’ve discarded the raw data and continued with the set of strengths, you can never see whether this particular connection had changed in an old vs young brain. You can only see specific changes in the strength or how they are topologically organised, *under some inductive bias*. You could **never** see if this connection suddenly became highly nonlinear in an old vs young brain — you could **never see that**. ***This is the core limitation*** .
    
- This is the primary thing: even though pyspi addresses one aspect of the problem (”*how do you pick a good measure of strength?*”), it still leaves you with a representation that can only see strength, that is only receptive and sensitive to strength. If you instead adopt existing methods of abstraction to represent the system, you still only see things that are sensitive to strength. It just tells you how they are topologically organised — this is not a bad thing! It’s great to see if strengths are topologically organised differently; it’s great if you can see if this brain region couples different between an old vs young network. It is not a limitation that you shouldn’t represent the system as a set of strengths; it is *what representing the data like this can’t do?* It cannot see if there was a certain connection that was reliably linear in old and reliably nonlinear in young, or if a young brain was had a reliably faster connection with $\Delta t = 20\rm{ms}$ versus a relatively slower connection in an old subject of $\Delta10\rm{ms}$ so the cross-correlation function peaks higher— none of these existing abstractions could ever see it, they could never be sensitive to it, because they start with **baking in an assumption underpinning the type of coupling.**
- The number one, the primary gap is what structure **can’t we see** when we choose to represent our system as the set of strengths.

**General narrative and flow, colloquially.**

- We really want to compare MTS.
- The characteristic thing about multivariate time series is how different parts of the complex system interact/couple.
- Current methods of representing the structure pick a measure of interaction, and look at the strength of interactions and how they change. This allows them to find differences between groups of people (young vs old) under some lens (e.g. correlation), or if they abstract to a network, they can find topological re-arrangements of the strengths.
- None of these methods can see changes in *how* different parts of the system interact, i.e., if they become more nonlinear, if the time lags change, or if the phase lag becomes different to the dependence on its own past through Granger causality or [etc.].
- None of these current representations of solving this important problem can see this type of change.
- So what do we do?

**How does our method address these limitations?**

**Developing the aim:** quantify mechanistic dependence.

- Hundreds, thousands of papers choose to represent the system as a set of strengths between pairs of nodes — but none of them can see the character. This is the crux/nugget of the limitations and is central to the motivation of this method.
- **We aim to develop a method that is sensitive to the *character of interaction,* not just the strength.**
- **Why?** Why would we want to measure a change in the character of how parts interact?
    - Everyone’s doing it this way [representing under a strength measure], they’ve found all these differences (e.g., old peoples’ front and back of brain interact less strongly than young people, old people have more clustered brain network structures) — are we missing character and it’s nothing useful or are we missing this and it’s useful?
    - If we can’t answer that question, we will never be able to know if the useful information lies within the character of those relationships, and not just in the strength. [partially in this direction we would want to pitch]
    - It is also more fundamental: physically, we would expect the nature of coupling to change. If you have a brain and you flood it with a neurotransmitter that slows all the couplings, you would expect to see that in the change of the nature of coupling; if you get old the white matter fibres are degraded, you would expect the time delays to increase between different parts of the brain. And that may also manifest in changes in the topology and structure, but the primary thing that it is doing — and the most interpretable — is to conclude that this connection became slower, this connection is more degraded and has all this noise in it, so it has become more *linear*.
    - We cannot ever obtain this conclusion from existing analyses (by choosing a representation of the system as the set of strengths), because it starts **by baking in a “*how“.*** You \emph{start} with *how* they interact, and everything downstream of that choice can never reflect *how* the interaction has changed. And yet, we expect all of these biological changes to affect how system elements interact, and yet our representations for them bake in one choice a priori and render them blind to a change in that relationship.
    - Furthermore, being sensitive to changes in the character of interaction is typically a much more directly interpretable thing [than digesting some distance measure (correlation or euclidean distance)] between entries within corresponding adjacency matrices. For example, labelling that in this old person, \emph{all} of their connections became more linear is more useful than identifying a set of all of the significant changes in an old vs young subject, identifying that they’ve all become weaker, and this big spaghetti [referencing a traditional adjacency matrix of correlations in a neural setting] of connections have all become stronger [one adjacency matrix vs another under a single statistical prior].
    - Do you want that [reading two numbers between two systems under the same metric], or do you want me to say “No, look, all the couplings are much more nonlinear now…all the time delays have increased…[etc]”. This distinction is significantly easier to map back to a biological experiment or casual mechanism than the more crude comparison between (traditionally covariance) matrices identifying which pairs of nodes, sensors, regions are stronger, which are weaker, under some baked-in statistical prior governing the mechanism through which they are acting (”how?”). What do you do, or what is actionable, as a biologist if someone sends me two random system adjacency matrices under some statistic (e.g. covariance matrices)? Contrastingly, this method is capable of asking more fundamental questions, and responding with more interpretable meaning and deeper physical significance: “this group has more nonlinear connections” can allow for a biologist to posit more targeting and correspondent theories, e.g. “maybe it’s because of this transmitter, maybe its because of [potential cause]…”.
    - Side question: would knowing the type of interaction actually give you so much more [in neuroscience, at least]?
        - Response to side question: maybe if all the white matter fibres get weaker, maybe the coupling gets weaker in general.
    - **The bottom line: we could just never ask that question!** Is the brain more nonlinear when you are young? Do the time delays get younger when you are older? Because everyone starts with this baked inductive bias a priori. So, there’s huge potential for being able to find new patterns, that are biologically interpretable, to understand that young people’s brains are more nonlinear, that older people have longer time delays — these are useful, human-interpretable statements that biologists [or correspondingly for non-neural systems] can test and experiment with. This is in contrast with more niche constructions, offering more abstract interpretations, such as signalling that this network has a slightly longer tail of the degree distribution or greater modularity [from network topology abstractions], or that specific system elements are more entropic at longer lags, which are much more peripheral entries to asking these questions and typically require prior knowledge of the problem structure, specific domain knowledge, or deliberate construction.
