## Introduction

Many of the properties that distinguish complex systems are properties of how their parts interact. Cognition, a climate teleconnection and systemic financial risk are not attributes of a single cortical region, ocean basin or asset [@bassett2017network; @tsonis2004architecture; @mantegna1999hierarchical]. Multivariate time series (MTS), simultaneous recordings of many interacting components, are the main empirical access to those interactions. Many scientific questions about such systems are therefore comparisons of MTS: a system before and after a change of dynamical regime, two systems that may share an organising principle despite different components and sizes, or, in applications, one population of recordings against another. Part of what distinguishes them lies in individual channels, but much of it lies in the dependence among channels [@friston2011functional; @runge2019inferring].

The prevailing way to make dependence comparable is to summarise it. A pairwise statistic, such as a correlation, coherence, Granger-causal or transfer-entropy estimate, or phase-locking index, is computed for every pair of channels. The resulting matrix of coupling strengths then stands in for the recording in all downstream analysis [@pereda2005nonlinear; @bastos2016tutorial]. When channels correspond across recordings, these matrices are compared entry by entry or through their geometry, as in connectome fingerprinting [@finn2015functional], dynamic functional connectivity [@hutchison2013dynamic] and Riemannian classification [@barachant2012multiclass; @dadi2019benchmarking]. When channels do not correspond, the matrix is abstracted further into mean coupling, edge-weight distributions or network topology [@bullmore2009complex; @rubinov2010complex], which brings its own sensitivities to network size and density [@vanwijk2010comparing; @tantardini2019comparing]. Multilayer approaches keep several such matrices, typically one per frequency band, as the layers of one network [@brookes2016multilayer; @buldu2018frequency]. Machine learning takes one of three routes. It infers an adjacency of interaction strengths [@wu2020connecting; @jin2024gnn4ts], discards cross-channel dependence by treating channels independently [@nie2023patchtst; @han2024capacity], or learns dependence end to end in coordinates that have no stated meaning [@liu2024itransformer; @woo2024moirai]. Across these traditions the primitive is the same: how strongly pairs of components are coupled, under one or a few chosen notions of coupling. Each further abstraction trades information for comparability, and none can restore what the first summary removed, because any function of two identical strength matrices is identical.

Hundreds of pairwise statistics exist because dependence has many facets. It can be linear or nonlinear, instantaneous or lagged, directed or symmetric, carried by phase or by amplitude, and confined to one frequency band or spread across several. Choosing a statistic fixes, before any data are seen, the sense in which components are taken to interact; the matrix then reports how strongly they interact in that sense. Pearson correlation measures linear, zero-lag association. The phase-lag index discards zero-phase coupling by design, to suppress volume conduction [@nolte2004identifying; @stam2007phase]. Transfer entropy depends on the history length it is given [@schreiber2000measuring; @wibral2013measuring]. Systematic comparisons find that conclusions about network organisation, structure–function correspondence, individual identification and behavioural prediction can change substantially with the statistic [@smith2011network; @wang2014systematic; @liu2025benchmarking]. The *pyspi* library extended the highly comparative approach of univariate time-series analysis [@fulcher2013highly; @fulcher2017hctsa] to pairwise dependence. Its more than two hundred statistics of pairwise interactions (SPIs) make the choice of statistic empirical rather than conventional [@cliff2023unifying], and across tasks the best and worst statistics differ widely in performance [@cliff2023unifying; @bryant2024extracting]. `[ALIGN: quote the published spread.]`

Choosing well does not change what is represented. A pairwise statistic compresses the relationship between two time series, a joint process with form, timing and spectral content, into one number. That map is many-to-one: relationships of very different form can share a correlation coefficient [@anscombe1973graphs], and correlation summarises dependence completely only for elliptical distributions [@embrechts2002correlation]. Two recordings can therefore share every zero-lag correlation while their components interact differently. In one, components move together without delay; in the other, they follow one another at delays that differ from pair to pair. Every analysis built on that matrix, whether entry-wise, spectral or topological, returns the same answer for both. A change in *how* components interact is therefore not invisible to a strength representation; it is aliased. A fixed statistic registers at most a change in its value, which cannot be distinguished from a change in coupling strength, and a better statistic changes which differences are aliased, not whether some are. Such differences occur in real systems. Between wakefulness and slow-wave sleep, networks defined by zero-lag correlation are largely preserved, while the lag structure of propagated activity reorganises [@mitra2015propagated]. Axonal conduction delays vary systematically across the human cortex [@lemarechal2022atlas] and continue to shorten into adulthood [@vanblooijs2023developmental]. Transitions between dynamical regimes are a further case. At a synchronisation transition the coupling law stays fixed, while the observed dependence between components reorganises, for example from intermittent phase slips to sustained locking [@arenas2008synchronization]. Statements about such changes, that interactions became more variably delayed or changed from intermittent to locked, map onto experiments more directly than a list of edges that strengthened or weakened. Neither kind of statement identifies mechanism on its own [@mehler2018lure; @reid2019advancing].

The character of dependence has not been ignored, but it has been interrogated one hypothesis at a time.
- *Nonlinearity.* Surrogate-data tests ask whether a dependence exceeds what a linear Gaussian process could produce [@theiler1992testing; @prichard1994generating; @schreiber2000surrogate]. Comparisons of linear with nonlinear redundancies [@palus1995testing], or of mutual information with its Gaussian counterpart, quantify nonlinear interdependence in electroencephalography [@breakspear2002detection] and functional magnetic resonance imaging [@hlinka2011functional]. Contrasts between linear and nonlinear dependence networks have characterised climate dynamics and financial crises [@donges2009complex; @haluszczynski2017linear].
- *Timing.* Cross-correlograms [@perkel1967neuronal], lag-structure analyses [@mitra2014lag] and delay-resolved transfer entropy [@wibral2013measuring] target delays.
- *Phase versus amplitude.* Phase- and amplitude-coupling patterns have been contrasted directly [@engel2013intrinsic; @siems2020dissociated; @mostame2020phase].
- *Redundancy versus synergy.* Information decomposition separates redundant from synergistic interaction [@luppi2022synergistic].

Each of these approaches detects a change of character only if the analyst already suspects which kind of change to test. Agreement between statistics has itself been studied in two ways. Analytically, it appears as fixed relations that hold under Gaussianity and whose violation signals non-Gaussian coupling [@nolte2020mathematical]. Empirically, it has been measured and averaged over recordings to characterise the statistics [@cliff2023unifying; @liu2025benchmarking], not to describe an individual recording. A vector of per-statistic summaries can register a change of character, but only as a shift in apparent strength, and any recording-wide change in gain or signal-to-noise ratio moves all of its entries together. What such summaries discard is which channel pairs score jointly high or low across statistics. That joint organisation is where character becomes visible, and to our knowledge it has not been used to represent a recording.

We propose such a representation by inverting the role of the statistic library: rather than selecting the best statistic, we measure how statistics relate within a recording. Each statistic's values over all channel pairs of a recording form its *edge profile*. For SPIs $a$ and $b$ computed on an $M$-channel recording, $z_{ab}$ is the correlation between their edge profiles across all $M(M-1)$ ordered off-diagonal entries, so both directions of every channel pair are retained. The vector of all $z_{ab}$ is the SPI–SPI representation. A coordinate $z_{ab}$ is high when two statistics order channel pairs alike. It falls when the property that distinguishes them varies across pairs independently of shared strength. If delays vary from pair to pair, Euclidean distance and its lag-tolerant counterpart disagree about which pairs are closest. If the form of dependence varies, Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation and mutual information disagree about which pairs are most dependent. The representation therefore targets how the character of dependence is organised across a system; a change applied uniformly to every pair registers only weakly.

Because $z_{ab}$ is unchanged by positive affine rescaling of either profile, recording-wide shifts in a statistic's level or spread do not register as change. Coupling strength can still affect $z$ through the shape of the profiles. For linear Gaussian data, many coupling measures are fixed functions of coherency [@nolte2020mathematical], so the spread of strength across pairs alone determines part of $z$. We therefore test readings against multivariate linear surrogates that preserve that spread [@prichard1994generating]. `[ALIGN: required analysis, not yet run.]` The construction also requires heterogeneity across pairs in both profiles, and it becomes ill-conditioned when a statistic saturates. Each coordinate is indexed by two statistics with documented assumptions, which proposes a candidate interpretation. As with any multivariate readout [@haufe2014interpretation], we accept that interpretation only after perturbation, surrogate and transfer tests.

Comparable second-order constructions compare neural representations [@kriegeskorte2008representational; @kornblith2019similarity] and the layers of multiplex networks [@nicosia2015measuring; @dedomenico2015structural]. The same across-edge correlation, applied between structural and functional connectivity, tracks brain development [@baum2020development]. Here the layers are alternative notions of dependence evaluated on one system.

The construction also addresses the comparability problem that motivated topological abstraction. The dimension of $z$ depends only on the number of statistics, not on the number of channels or samples. Recordings of different sizes, sensor layouts and systems therefore share one coordinate system without channel correspondence. A shared coordinate system does not by itself make positions comparable: estimates depend on recording length and channel count, and sampling must be matched to each system's timescales. We quantify these dependencies. `[ALIGN]`

We demonstrate the representation in four settings of increasing breadth:
- **Constructed cases.** Its coordinates read named axes of character. Agreement among Pearson correlation, Spearman correlation and mutual information follows the form of dependence from linear through monotone to non-monotone. Agreement among Euclidean, lag-tolerant Euclidean and dynamic-time-warping distances follows whether components are aligned, shifted or warped in time.
- **Generator classes.** Recordings from ten generator classes, each simulated with 8, 16 or 32 channels and 500 to 2,000 samples, share one space. Classes separate there along selected feature axes whose joint reading is consistent with the generating processes.
- **Corpus archive.** Across an archive of 1,053 synthetic and real multivariate time series, previously used to organise the statistics themselves [@cliff2023unifying], processes of different origin and appearance occupy common regions. We read these regions through the statistics that place them there.
- **Regime transitions.** Without state labels, the leading principal component of the representation tracks transitions between dynamical regimes, including the onset of cross-frequency locking. `[ALIGN: systems shown.]`

Principal components of raw configurations can recover order parameters in spin systems [@wang2016discovering]. Here, by contrast, the coordinate is built from named statistics of dependence and is defined identically for every system. In each setting we compare against summaries of strength, such as mean absolute correlation and per-statistic means. `[ALIGN: report where these suffice.]`

SPI–SPI introduces no new estimator; it draws on the field's accumulated methodology. We close by outlining targeted measures that would place each channel pair on named axes of character rather than of strength. Examples are linear to nonlinear, fixed to variable lag, and phase- to amplitude-mediated.
